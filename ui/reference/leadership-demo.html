    Confidence check

    Jev · built to measure certainty

    A model built for one thing: returning calibrated probabilities rather than text. Calibrated means the odds mean what they say — when it reports 90%, it is right about nine times in ten. It gives a probability for each type, a single certainty score derived from how concentrated those odds are, and a separate yes/no per type so it can report "none of these" or "more than one." General-purpose models cannot do this dependably; this one does only this.

    Separate yes/no check per type
    Certainty score
    —

    Derived from the shape of the odds: high when they concentrate on one type, low when they spread. Because the odds are calibrated, this score can be trusted as a threshold. Every decision rule leans on it.

    Jev

    The confidence check

    Its job is not speed; speed is a by-product. It is trained to return calibrated probabilities — odds that mean what they say — and a certainty score derived from them. That score is what lets the rules decide when to trust an answer and when to ask a person. Ordinary AI models produce fluent answers but cannot reliably tell you how sure they are.

    The same general-purpose model, briefed five different ways. Each knows one report type deeply, including what its nearest look-alike is, and reads the whole document before answering.

    The decision

    Ordinary code. No model picks the final label. Agreement between two unrelated systems is the evidence; disagreement is exactly what a person should see.

    Why two systems instead of one?

    A single model always produces an answer and has no dependable way to signal how much to trust it. Two systems built by different companies, trained on different objectives, and reading different amounts of the document rarely fail the same way. When they agree, that agreement is the confidence. When they don't, a person decides.

    Why not just use the stronger model for everything?

    Because it cannot tell you when it is guessing. A general-purpose model gives a fluent answer whether it is 99% sure or 51% sure, and asking it to rate its own confidence produces a number that is not reliable. The confidence check is trained for exactly that measurement and nothing else — its probabilities are calibrated, so a threshold on them means something. And because it comes from a different company with a different design, it is a genuinely independent second opinion. One model checking itself is not.

    What happens to reports a person reviews?

    The reviewer sees both opinions and the readers' one-line reasons, picks the label, and that label is saved. Over time those decisions become the examples the readers are briefed with, so the system gets better on exactly the cases it found hard. A recurring "no match" is how a new report type gets discovered.
